Welcome to my website!

Pharmaceutical science student at Monash, working on how the ageing body pushes the brain toward dementia.

Microglia (green) and neurons (red), rat brain culture. GerryShaw / EnCor Biotechnology, CC BY-SA 3.0

My name is Ziyao Qing. I am a Dean's Scholar in the Bachelor of Pharmaceutical Science Advanced (Honours) Scholars Program at the Monash Institute of Pharmaceutical Sciences (MIPS), Monash University, Melbourne.

I want to understand why the ageing brain becomes vulnerable to dementia, and to help turn that understanding into new medicines. My interests sit between neuroscience and pharmacology: microglia and neuroinflammation, tau pathology, the brain's clearance systems and, more and more, the link between the brain and the bone marrow that supplies its immune cells.

In 2021, an estimated 57 million people worldwide were living with dementia (WHO), and treatments that change the course of the disease are still scarce. I think of dementia as more than a disease of the brain. If we learn how the brain's clearance, immune and organelle systems fail with age, and how the rest of the body, especially the blood-forming system, contributes, we should find new ways to slow or prevent neurodegeneration.

Working on haematopoietic stem cells in the Arimura laboratory, I keep returning to one question: could part of what goes wrong in the ageing brain start in the bone marrow? Recent work suggests that from around age 50, the brain's microglia are gradually replaced by cells made in the marrow, and that in amyloid mice, chronic interferon signalling disrupts that supply (Abellanas et al., Nature Neuroscience 2026). The idea is new and rests mostly on mouse data. But if the brain and the blood-forming system influence each other in both directions, that link could be a new place to intervene.

So far I have trained in four laboratories in Japan, each working on neurodegeneration from a different angle: the Alzheimer's-related kinase MARK4, inflammation in the ageing brain, contacts between organelles, and the role of blood stem cells in dementia. Each project, and what I am thinking about now, is described on the Research page.
