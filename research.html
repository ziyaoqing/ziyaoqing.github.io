How might an ageing body push the brain toward dementia?

Dementia rarely has a single cause. I want to understand the steps that link an ageing body to a failing brain, and which of them a medicine could reach. Since late 2025 I have worked in four laboratories in Japan, each on a different part of the problem.

  • Jan – Feb 2026Ageing and senoinflammationInstitute of Science Tokyo
  • Dec 2025 – Jan 2026MARK4 and early tauTokyo Metropolitan University

Research interests

Brain–bone marrow axis

How ageing and disease change blood formation in the bone marrow, including the skull marrow next to the brain; how that changes the immune cells reaching the brain; and whether the marrow could be reset to protect it.

Glia and the neurovascular unit

How microglia, astrocytes, pericytes and blood vessels work with neurons, and how their crosstalk drives inflammation, amyloid-β and tau pathology, and cognitive decline.

Proteostasis and brain clearance

How amyloid-β and tau are modified and build up, and how the brain removes them through protein degradation, the glymphatic system and meningeal and vascular routes.

From mechanism to medicine

Turning these mechanisms into early biomarkers and treatments, such as glia-targeted drugs, protection of the neurovascular unit and cell-based approaches.

The grants and publications listed under each placement belong to the host laboratories. I list them as background to each theme; they are not my own awards or papers, and some appeared after my placement ended.

Sep 2026 – presentTohoku University
Sendai, Japan

Bone marrow, blood and the ageing brain

Haematopoietic stem cells and the origins of dementia
Laboratory of Pharmacology, Graduate School of Pharmaceutical Sciences. Supervisor: Assoc. Prof. Nariko Arimura

In the host lab

What first sets dementia in motion is still unknown. Assoc. Prof. Arimura’s laboratory looks for part of the answer in the blood: in haematopoietic (blood-forming) stem cells, the cells they make and the molecules those cells release. By their forties, almost everyone with Down syndrome has the brain changes of Alzheimer’s disease. The lab found that normal mice given bone marrow from a Down syndrome model develop cognitive decline, even though their brains are genetically normal. The team has isolated plasma metabolites and exosomes that change in these mice and is now asking which of them reach the brain. It is also studying how young bone marrow changes the ageing brain.

My placement

I joined the laboratory in September 2026 as a part-time undergraduate research assistant.

Current thinking

In July 2026, two studies of human brain tissue reported that from around age 50, microglia are gradually replaced by cells made in the bone marrow, and that by about 80 almost all of them are (Alzforum, Aug 2026). Florent Ginhoux called the blood system “a previously underappreciated contributor” to microglial biology. If that holds, the quality of what the marrow produces matters for the ageing brain. Whether the newcomers help or harm is open. In one study, people whose brains carried marrow-derived clones had about half the odds of Alzheimer’s disease; in the other, the marrow-derived microglia were more inflammatory.

In September, Michal Schwartz’s group showed that in amyloid mice, chronic type I interferon disrupts monocyte production in the marrow, and that blocking it restored the supply and protected neurons (Alzforum, Sep 2026). Together with the Arimura lab’s Down syndrome result, this looks to me like a loop: the diseased brain may reshape the marrow, and the marrow may in turn act on the brain. Nathan Zemke, who led one of the human studies, calls the idea that marrow-derived cells shape the disease “an emerging concept rather than an established paradigm”, and most of the evidence for the loop still comes from mice. That is the part I want to help test.

Jun – Aug 2026Shimane University
Izumo, Japan

ER–mitochondria contacts in neurodegeneration

Organelle-contact abnormalities in neurodegenerative disease
Department of Life Science (Chemistry), Faculty of Medicine. Supervisor: Assoc. Prof. Seiji Watanabe

In the host lab

Protein build-up, failing mitochondria and inflammation are often studied separately. One place where they meet is the mitochondria-associated membrane (MAM), the contact site between the endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria. The MAM handles Ca2+ transfer, lipid synthesis, mitochondrial division, autophagy and responses to proteostatic stress. Assoc. Prof. Watanabe and colleagues have shown that the MAM is disrupted in several forms of ALS, and that losing it impairs the TBK1-mediated response to proteostatic stress. The lab now asks which parts of the MAM are damaged in disease, whether microglia have MAM defects too, and whether restoring the MAM can reduce neuroinflammation.

My placement

My project was Investigation of novel organelle-contact abnormalities using neurodegenerative disease models. Using the lab’s cell systems and stored samples, it compared how much MAM forms, where it forms and which proteins sit there in disease models and controls, and related those differences to mitochondrial shape, proteostatic stress and neuroinflammation.

Current thinking

In July 2026, researchers described “mitochondrial plaques”: large clusters of mitochondria-filled dystrophic neurites next to amyloid plaques, in mouse models and in Alzheimer’s brain (Alzforum, Aug 2026). Ralph Nixon read them as one sign of a wider collapse of autophagy in vulnerable neurons. I am more interested in what happens before the pile-up. The MAM is one of the places where mitochondria are divided, checked and handed over to autophagy, so could failure at the contact site come first? The idea is not new. TDP-43 was shown to loosen the ER–mitochondria link back in 2014 (Alzforum, Jun 2014), but only now are there good tools to watch these contacts in tissue. For me the useful question is where two organelles stop working together.

Jan – Feb 2026Institute of Science Tokyo
Tokyo, Japan

Ageing, senescent glia and inflammation

Ageing-induced senoinflammation in dementia
Department of Pharmacology, Graduate School of Medical and Dental Sciences. Supervisor: Prof. Tomoyuki Furuyashiki

In the host lab

Age is the biggest risk factor for dementia. Part of the reason may be senescent cells, which stop dividing but do not die, and keep releasing inflammatory signals (“senoinflammation”) into the tissue around them. Prof. Furuyashiki’s laboratory studies how ageing and chronic stress change brain circuits through inflammation in the brain and the rest of the body. In mice, the lab has shown that repeated stress shifts the bone marrow toward making neutrophils and monocytes and raises their numbers in the blood; such cells are thought to reach the brain and contribute to stress-induced behaviour. This is the same body-to-brain route I now work on at Tohoku. More recently, the lab found that oxidative stress in synaptic mitochondria of the prefrontal cortex helps explain why some aged mice lose cognitive flexibility and others don’t. The lab takes part in two Moonshot Goal 7 projects on preventing and controlling dementia.

My placement

I trained in the lab on this theme in January and February 2026.

Current thinking

Senolytics, drugs that clear senescent cells, have been tried in small Alzheimer’s studies. In a five-person pilot, dasatinib reached the cerebrospinal fluid, but biomarkers and cognition did not change over 12 weeks; a placebo-controlled trial in 48 people is listed to run until January 2027 (Alzforum therapeutics database). In August 2026, Li Gan’s group reported that in mice with shortened telomeres, a model of accelerated ageing, senescent microglia release a protein, DLK1, that disrupts myelin and neuronal signalling (Alzforum, Aug 2026). Rahul Nagvekar noted that myelin breakdown has long been linked to neurodegeneration but rarely targeted, and this study names a candidate driver. That suggests another strategy: block the specific factors senescent cells release, instead of removing the cells. It also helps me see why the Moonshot project puts so much weight on imaging senoinflammation: a drug target is only useful if we can see whether a treatment reaches it.

Dec 2025 – Jan 2026Tokyo Metropolitan University
Tokyo, Japan

MARK4 and early tau toxicity

The role of MARK4 in neuroinflammation
Laboratory of Molecular Neuroscience, Graduate School of Science. Supervisor: Prof. Kanae Ando

In the host lab

Tau seems to become toxic well before it forms tangles, and the enzymes that modify it early are possible drug targets. Prof. Ando’s laboratory studies MARK4, a kinase linked to Alzheimer’s disease by both genetics and pathology. The lab has shown that deleting Mark4 in a tauopathy mouse model reduces pathological tau, neurodegeneration and astrogliosis, and improves memory and survival. It has also developed a cell-penetrating peptide that blocks MARK4 and reduces tau toxicity. Separately, the lab studies how neuronal and glial metabolism shape brain ageing: in a fly model of tau toxicity, raising glucose uptake in glia reduced neuroinflammation and protected neurons.

My placement

I studied the role of MARK4 in neuroinflammation, supported by an Undergraduate Research Scholarship from Tokyo Metropolitan University.

Current thinking

In 2025, Thomas Karikari’s group characterised the soluble tau assemblies that form before tangles. Their core is phosphorylated at Ser262 and Ser356, and these species in cerebrospinal fluid tracked tangle load and cognitive decline (Alzforum, Feb 2025). These are the sites that MARK-family kinases phosphorylate. The Ando lab’s fly work complicates the picture, though: MARK1, 2 and 4 all increased phosphorylation at these sites, but only MARK4 made tau accumulate, so the sites alone do not explain toxicity. In 2016, halving the level of Nuak1, a related kinase that acts at Ser356, also reduced tau pathology in mice, and Bingwei Lu suggested this kinase family as a new way into the tauopathies (Alzforum, Oct 2016). I would like to know whether the same early tau species could work both as an early warning and as a readout for MARK4-targeted drugs.

How the four fit together

  1. Bone marrow outputTohoku
  2. Senescent gliaScience Tokyo
  3. Organelle contactsShimane
  4. Early tauTokyo Metropolitan

These were four separate projects, but read in order they trace one possible route from the body to the neuron. The bone marrow supplies some of the brain’s immune cells, and its output changes with age and disease. Those cells, and the brain’s own glia, can become senescent and inflamed. Inside stressed neurons and glia, the contacts between organelles that keep mitochondria and protein handling in balance begin to fail. At the end of the chain is tau, modified by kinases such as MARK4 into forms that damage synapses. I do not think any single step is the cause of dementia. What interests me is the order in which these steps fail, and where a medicine could still intervene.
