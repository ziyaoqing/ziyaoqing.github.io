How an ageing body pushes the brain toward dementia, and where we might step in.

I want to understand why the ageing brain becomes vulnerable to dementia. I am interested in how the brain's immune, clearance and organelle systems wear down with age, how the rest of the body (the bone marrow in particular) adds to that decline, and which of these steps could be treated with drugs. Since late 2025 I have worked in four laboratories in Japan, each on a different part of the problem.

Brain–bone marrow axis

How ageing and disease change blood formation in the bone marrow, including the skull marrow next to the brain; how that changes the immune cells reaching the brain; and whether the marrow could be reset to protect it.

Glia and the neurovascular unit

How microglia, astrocytes, pericytes and blood vessels work with neurons, and how their crosstalk drives inflammation, amyloid-β and tau pathology, and cognitive decline.

Proteostasis and brain clearance

How amyloid-β and tau are modified and build up, and how the brain removes them through protein degradation, the glymphatic system and meningeal and vascular routes.

From mechanism to medicine

Turning these mechanisms into early biomarkers and treatments, such as glia-targeted drugs, protection of the neurovascular unit and cell-based approaches.

Bone marrow, blood and the ageing brain

Haematopoietic stem cells and the origins of dementia
Laboratory of Pharmacology, Graduate School of Pharmaceutical Sciences. Supervisor: Assoc. Prof. Nariko Arimura

In the host lab. We still don't know what first sets dementia in motion, and the blood, both its cells and what they secrete, turns out to have a strong influence on the brain. Assoc. Prof. Arimura's laboratory studies how changes in haematopoietic (blood-forming) stem cells affect brain function. Almost everyone with Down syndrome develops early-onset Alzheimer's disease. The lab found that normal mice given bone marrow from a Down syndrome model develop cognitive decline, even though their brains are genetically normal. The team is now following the plasma metabolites and exosomes from these blood cells that reach the brain, and testing how young bone marrow changes the ageing brain.

My placement. I joined this project in September 2026 as an undergraduate research assistant.

Current thinking

In mid-2026, two studies reported that from around age 50, microglia in the human brain are gradually replaced by cells made in the bone marrow, and that by about 80 almost all of them are (Alzforum, Aug 2026). Florent Ginhoux called the blood system "a previously underappreciated contributor" to microglial biology. If that holds, the quality of what the marrow produces matters for the ageing brain. A month later, Michal Schwartz's group showed that in amyloid mice, chronic type I interferon disrupts monocyte production in the marrow, and that blocking it protected neurons (Alzforum, Sep 2026). Taken together with the Arimura lab's Down syndrome result, this looks to me like a loop: the diseased brain may reshape the marrow, and the marrow feeds back on the brain. Nathan Zemke calls it "an emerging concept rather than an established paradigm", and most of the evidence still comes from mice. That is the part I want to help test.

ER–mitochondria contacts in neurodegeneration

Organelle-contact abnormalities in neurodegenerative disease
Department of Life Science (Chemistry), Faculty of Medicine. Supervisor: Assoc. Prof. Seiji Watanabe

In the host lab. Protein build-up, failing mitochondria and inflammation are usually studied separately. One place where they meet is the mitochondria-associated membrane (MAM), the contact site between the endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria. The MAM handles Ca2+ transfer, lipid synthesis, mitochondrial division, autophagy and responses to proteostatic stress. Assoc. Prof. Watanabe and colleagues have shown that MAM disruption is common to several ALS-causing genes, and that losing the MAM impairs the TBK1-mediated response to proteostatic stress. The lab now asks which parts of the MAM are damaged in disease, whether microglia have MAM defects too, and whether restoring the MAM can reduce neuroinflammation.

My placement. My project, Investigation of novel organelle-contact abnormalities using neurodegenerative disease models, compared MAM formation, location and related proteins between disease models and controls, using the lab's cell systems and stored samples, and related these changes to mitochondrial shape, proteostatic stress and neuroinflammation.

Current thinking

In August 2026, researchers described "mitochondrial plaques": large clusters of mitochondria-filled dystrophic neurites next to amyloid plaques, in mouse models and in Alzheimer's brain (Alzforum, Aug 2026). Ralph Nixon read them as one sign of a wider collapse of autophagy in vulnerable neurons. I keep coming at this from the other side. The MAM is one of the places where mitochondria are divided, checked and handed over to autophagy, so could failure at the contact site come before the pile-up? The idea is not new. TDP-43 was shown to loosen the ER–mitochondria link back in 2014 (Alzforum, Jun 2014), but only now are there good tools to watch these contacts in tissue. I find it useful to ask not only which organelle breaks, but where two organelles stop working together.

Jan – Feb 2026Institute of Science Tokyo
Tokyo, Japan

Ageing, senescent glia and inflammation

Ageing-induced senoinflammation in dementia
Department of Pharmacology, Graduate School of Medical and Dental Sciences. Supervisor: Prof. Tomoyuki Furuyashiki

In the host lab. Age is the biggest risk factor for dementia. Part of the reason may be senescent cells, which stop dividing but do not die, and keep releasing inflammatory signals ("senoinflammation") into the tissue around them. Prof. Furuyashiki's laboratory studies how ageing and chronic stress change brain circuits through inflammation in the brain and the rest of the body. The lab has shown that long-term stress mobilises cells from the bone marrow into the brain, where they contribute to behavioural change. This is the same body-to-brain route I now work on at Tohoku. More recently, the lab found that oxidative stress in synaptic mitochondria of the prefrontal cortex helps explain why some aged mice lose cognitive flexibility and others don't. The lab takes part in two Moonshot Goal 7 projects on preventing and controlling dementia.

My placement. I trained in the lab on this theme in January and February 2026.

Current thinking

Senolytics, drugs that clear senescent cells, have been tried in small Alzheimer's studies. Dasatinib reached the cerebrospinal fluid, but biomarkers and cognition did not change over 12 weeks; a larger placebo-controlled trial is expected to run into 2027 (Alzforum therapeutics database). Meanwhile, Li Gan's group found that senescent microglia in a mouse model of ageing release one factor, DLK1, that disrupts myelin and neuronal signalling (Alzforum, Aug 2026). Rahul Nagvekar noted that myelin breakdown has long been linked to neurodegeneration but rarely targeted, and this study names a candidate driver. It points to a different strategy: rather than removing senescent cells, block the specific factors that do the damage. It also explains why the Moonshot project puts so much weight on imaging senoinflammation. A drug target is only useful if we can see whether we are hitting it.

Dec 2025 – Jan 2026Tokyo Metropolitan University

MARK4 and early tau toxicity

The role of MARK4 in neuroinflammation
Laboratory of Molecular Neuroscience, Graduate School of Science. Supervisor: Prof. Kanae Ando

In the host lab. Tau becomes toxic well before it forms tangles, and the enzymes that modify it early are possible drug targets. Prof. Ando's laboratory studies MARK4, a kinase linked to Alzheimer's disease by both genetics and pathology. The lab has shown that deleting Mark4 in a tauopathy mouse model reduces pathological tau, neurodegeneration and astrogliosis, and improves memory and survival. It has also developed a cell-penetrating peptide that blocks MARK4 and reduces tau toxicity. Separately, the lab studies how neuronal and glial metabolism shape brain ageing: in a fly model of tau toxicity, raising glucose uptake in glia reduced neuroinflammation and protected neurons.

My placement. I studied the role of MARK4 in neuroinflammation, supported by an Undergraduate Research Scholarship from Tokyo Metropolitan University.

Current thinking

In 2025, Thomas Karikari's group characterised the soluble tau assemblies that form before tangles. Their core is phosphorylated at serines 262 and 356, and these species in cerebrospinal fluid tracked tangle load and cognitive decline (Alzforum, Feb 2025). These are the sites that MARK-family kinases phosphorylate. The Ando lab's fly work complicates the picture, though: MARK1, 2 and 4 all increased phosphorylation at these sites, but only MARK4 made tau accumulate, so the sites alone do not explain toxicity. In 2016, blocking Nuak1, a related kinase that acts at Ser356, also reduced tau pathology in mice, and Bingwei Lu suggested this kinase family as a new way into the tauopathies (Alzforum, Oct 2016). I would like to know whether the same early tau species could work both as an early warning and as a readout for MARK4-targeted drugs.
