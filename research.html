How do brain and body shape dementia?

I want to understand why the ageing brain becomes vulnerable to dementia. I am interested in how the brain's immune, clearance and organelle systems wear down with age, how the rest of the body (the bone marrow in particular) adds to that decline, and which of these steps could be treated with drugs. Since late 2025 I have worked in four laboratories in Japan, each on a different part of the problem.

Brain–bone marrow axis

How ageing and disease change blood formation in the bone marrow, including the skull marrow next to the brain; how that changes the immune cells reaching the brain; and whether the marrow could be reset to protect it.

Glia and the neurovascular unit

How microglia, astrocytes, pericytes and blood vessels work with neurons, and how their crosstalk drives inflammation, amyloid-β and tau pathology, and cognitive decline.

Proteostasis and brain clearance

How amyloid-β and tau are modified and build up, and how the brain removes them through protein degradation, the glymphatic system and meningeal and vascular routes.

From mechanism to medicine

Turning these mechanisms into early biomarkers and treatments, such as glia-targeted drugs, protection of the neurovascular unit and cell-based approaches.

Bone marrow, blood and the ageing brain

Haematopoietic stem cells and the origins of dementia
Laboratory of Pharmacology, Graduate School of Pharmaceutical Sciences. Supervisor: Assoc. Prof. Nariko Arimura

In the host lab

Assoc. Prof. Arimura’s laboratory studies how haematopoietic (blood-forming) stem cells and blood-derived signals relate to brain function. This placement connects my interest in neurodegeneration with the question of how changes outside the brain may influence cognition.

My placement

I joined this project in September 2026 as an undergraduate research assistant.

Current thinking

Could a persistent change in the blood-forming system alter synaptic function in an otherwise normal brain? I want to distinguish peripheral output, the signals that reach the brain, and the brain’s response. These are research questions, and evidence for one link does not establish the whole causal chain.

Jun – Aug 2026Shimane University
Izumo, Japan

ER–mitochondria contacts in neurodegeneration

Organelle-contact abnormalities in neurodegenerative disease
Department of Life Science (Chemistry), Faculty of Medicine. Supervisor: Assoc. Prof. Seiji Watanabe

In the host lab

Protein build-up, failing mitochondria and inflammation are usually studied separately. One place where they meet is the mitochondria-associated membrane (MAM), the contact site between the endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria. The MAM handles Ca2+ transfer, lipid synthesis, mitochondrial division, autophagy and responses to proteostatic stress. Assoc. Prof. Watanabe and colleagues have shown that MAM disruption is common to several ALS-causing genes, and that losing the MAM impairs the TBK1-mediated response to proteostatic stress. The lab now asks which parts of the MAM are damaged in disease, whether microglia have MAM defects too, and whether restoring the MAM can reduce neuroinflammation.

My placement

My project, Investigation of novel organelle-contact abnormalities using neurodegenerative disease models, compared MAM formation, location and related proteins between disease models and controls, using the lab's cell systems and stored samples, and related these changes to mitochondrial shape, proteostatic stress and neuroinflammation.

Current thinking

In August 2026, researchers described "mitochondrial plaques": large clusters of mitochondria-filled dystrophic neurites next to amyloid plaques, in mouse models and in Alzheimer's brain (Alzforum, Aug 2026). Ralph Nixon read them as one sign of a wider collapse of autophagy in vulnerable neurons. I keep coming at this from the other side. The MAM is one of the places where mitochondria are divided, checked and handed over to autophagy, so could failure at the contact site come before the pile-up? The idea is not new. TDP-43 was shown to loosen the ER–mitochondria link back in 2014 (Alzforum, Jun 2014), but only now are there good tools to watch these contacts in tissue. I find it useful to ask not only which organelle breaks, but where two organelles stop working together.

Jan – Feb 2026Institute of Science Tokyo
Tokyo, Japan

Ageing, senescent glia and inflammation

Ageing-induced senoinflammation in dementia
Department of Pharmacology, Graduate School of Medical and Dental Sciences. Supervisor: Prof. Tomoyuki Furuyashiki

In the host lab

Age is the biggest risk factor for dementia. Part of the reason may be senescent cells, which stop dividing but do not die, and keep releasing inflammatory signals ("senoinflammation") into the tissue around them. Prof. Furuyashiki's laboratory studies how ageing and chronic stress change brain circuits through inflammation in the brain and the rest of the body. The lab has shown that long-term stress mobilises cells from the bone marrow into the brain, where they contribute to behavioural change. This is the same body-to-brain route I now work on at Tohoku. More recently, the lab found that oxidative stress in synaptic mitochondria of the prefrontal cortex helps explain why some aged mice lose cognitive flexibility and others don't. The lab takes part in two Moonshot Goal 7 projects on preventing and controlling dementia.

My placement

I trained in the lab on this theme in January and February 2026.

Current thinking

Senolytics, drugs that clear senescent cells, have been tried in small Alzheimer's studies. Dasatinib reached the cerebrospinal fluid, but biomarkers and cognition did not change over 12 weeks; a larger placebo-controlled trial is expected to run into 2027 (Alzforum therapeutics database). Meanwhile, Li Gan's group found that senescent microglia in a mouse model of ageing release one factor, DLK1, that disrupts myelin and neuronal signalling (Alzforum, Aug 2026). Rahul Nagvekar noted that myelin breakdown has long been linked to neurodegeneration but rarely targeted, and this study names a candidate driver. It points to a different strategy: rather than removing senescent cells, block the specific factors that do the damage. It also explains why the Moonshot project puts so much weight on imaging senoinflammation. A drug target is only useful if we can see whether we are hitting it.

Dec 2025 – Jan 2026Tokyo Metropolitan University
Tokyo, Japan

MARK4 and early tau toxicity

The role of MARK4 in neuroinflammation
Laboratory of Molecular Neuroscience, Graduate School of Science. Supervisor: Prof. Kanae Ando

In the host lab

Tau becomes toxic well before it forms tangles, and the enzymes that modify it early are possible drug targets. Prof. Ando's laboratory studies MARK4, a kinase linked to Alzheimer's disease by both genetics and pathology. The lab has shown that deleting Mark4 in a tauopathy mouse model reduces pathological tau, neurodegeneration and astrogliosis, and improves memory and survival. It has also developed a cell-penetrating peptide that blocks MARK4 and reduces tau toxicity. Separately, the lab studies how neuronal and glial metabolism shape brain ageing: in a fly model of tau toxicity, raising glucose uptake in glia reduced neuroinflammation and protected neurons.

My placement

I studied the role of MARK4 in neuroinflammation, supported by an Undergraduate Research Scholarship from Tokyo Metropolitan University.

Current thinking

In 2025, Thomas Karikari's group characterised the soluble tau assemblies that form before tangles. Their core is phosphorylated at serines 262 and 356, and these species in cerebrospinal fluid tracked tangle load and cognitive decline (Alzforum, Feb 2025). These are the sites that MARK-family kinases phosphorylate. The Ando lab's fly work complicates the picture, though: MARK1, 2 and 4 all increased phosphorylation at these sites, but only MARK4 made tau accumulate, so the sites alone do not explain toxicity. In 2016, blocking Nuak1, a related kinase that acts at Ser356, also reduced tau pathology in mice, and Bingwei Lu suggested this kinase family as a new way into the tauopathies (Alzforum, Oct 2016). I would like to know whether the same early tau species could work both as an early warning and as a readout for MARK4-targeted drugs.

How the four fit together

  1. Bone marrow outputTohoku
  2. Senescent gliaScience Tokyo
  3. Organelle contactsShimane
  4. Early tauTokyo Metropolitan

Read in order, the four placements trace one route from the body to the neuron. The bone marrow supplies the brain's immune cells, and its output changes with age and disease. Those cells, and the brain's own glia, can slip into a senescent, inflammatory state. Inside stressed neurons and glia, the contacts between organelles that keep mitochondria and proteostasis in balance start to fail. And at the end of that chain sits tau, modified by kinases such as MARK4 into the species that damage synapses. I do not think any one of these steps is "the cause" of dementia. What interests me is the order in which they fall, and which of them a medicine could still hold up.
